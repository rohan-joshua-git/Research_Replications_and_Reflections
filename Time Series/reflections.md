# Granger and Newbold (1974): Spurious Regressions in Econometrics

## The puzzle they start with

The paper opens with an observation that is easy to skim past: applied econometrics papers routinely report regressions with a very high $R^2$, sometimes $0.99$ or higher, sitting right next to a very low Durbin-Watson statistic, sometimes as low as $0.09$. $R^2$ is the share of variance in the dependent variable explained by the regressors. The Durbin-Watson statistic,

$$d = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}(e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_t^2}$$

is a check for whether the regression's residuals $e_t$ are autocorrelated, meaning today's error resembles yesterday's error rather than being independent noise. Using the approximation $d \approx 2(1-\rho)$ where $\rho$ is the first order autocorrelation of the residuals, $d$ near $2$ means well behaved noise, and $d$ near $0$ means the residuals are drifting in long slow swings rather than bouncing randomly.

So the pattern they are flagging is a regression that looks like an excellent fit by $R^2$, but whose leftover error is clearly not noise at all. There are three known consequences of autocorrelated errors: inefficient coefficient estimates, sub-optimal forecasts, and invalid significance tests. The first two are already well understood. This entire paper is about the third, and its claim is blunt: once the errors are autocorrelated, the very tests used to call a relationship significant can no longer be trusted, and the relationship might be a complete fabrication, a spurious regression.

## Why economic time series behave this way

A stationary series bounces around a fixed mean forever, with constant variance and a fixed pattern of correlation with its own past. Such a series is commonly modeled as a mixed autoregressive moving average process,

$$\phi(B)W_t = \theta(B)a_t$$

where the autoregressive part says today depends on the series' own past values, and the moving average part says today depends on past random shocks $a_t$ that have not fully worked their way out yet. Most real economic series are not stationary in their raw level, they drift, but Box and Jenkins' insight is that differencing a series enough times, $d$ times, often reduces it to something stationary. A series requiring $d$ rounds of differencing is called ARIMA$(p,d,q)$, the I standing for integrated, because reversing $d$ rounds of differencing means summing, the discrete analogue of an integral.

Granger and Newbold's key empirical claim is that for economic series, $d=1$ is very often the right amount, equivalently that the autoregressive root of the raw level is $(1-\phi B)$ with $\phi$ extremely close to one. This is the boundary case called a unit root. Writing an AR(1) process as $(1-\phi B)X_t = a_t$, the root of the characteristic polynomial is $B = 1/\phi$. Stationarity needs that root outside the unit circle, meaning $|\phi| < 1$. When $\phi = 1$ exactly, a shock never decays, unrolling the recursion gives $X_t = X_0 + a_1 + \dots + a_t$, a random walk, whose variance grows with $t$ rather than settling down. Economic series being near unit root means shocks decay so slowly that over any realistic sample they behave almost like a true random walk, wandering in long persistent swings rather than reverting quickly to a mean.

## Why the classical significance tests break down

The usual regression $Y = X\beta + \varepsilon$ assumes $E(\varepsilon) = 0$ and $E(\varepsilon\varepsilon') = \sigma^2 I$, no autocorrelation, and the F-test for whether the regressors matter at all is only valid under that assumption. Suppose the null hypothesis $H_0: \beta_1 = \dots = \beta_{K-1} = 0$ is actually true, meaning there is genuinely no relationship. Substituting the zero coefficients into the model collapses it to $Y_t = \beta_0 + \varepsilon_t$, so the residual is just $\varepsilon_t = Y_t - \beta_0$, the series shifted by a constant. Whatever autocorrelation $Y_t$ has, the residual inherits completely, since subtracting a constant changes nothing about the series' internal dynamics. So exactly when $H_0$ is true and economic data is near unit root, the F-test's own assumptions are already violated.

They quantify this for the case where $Y_t$ and $X_t$ are independent AR(1) processes with parameters $\phi$ and $\phi^*$. Here $R^2$ reduces to the squared ordinary correlation between the two series, and Kendall's result gives

$$\text{Var}(R) = \frac{1}{T}\cdot\frac{1+\phi\phi^*}{1-\phi\phi^*}$$

Since $R$ is confined to $(-1,1)$, once this variance exceeds $1/3$ the distribution of $R$ can no longer be a single hump centered at zero. At $T=20$ and $\phi=\phi^*=0.9$, the expected $R^2$ comes out to $0.47$, an average outcome, not a rare fluke. Two genuinely unrelated series, each simply wandering on its own, will on average look like they explain nearly half of each other's variance, because two persistent series that each go on long slow excursions above and below their own mean will, over any given finite window, sometimes coincidentally overlap in direction, and a correlation coefficient has no way to distinguish that coincidence from a real link. Crucially this is not a search or cherry picking problem, it happens on a single honest one shot regression between one $Y$ and one $X$.

## The simulations

Section 4 turns this into a direct demonstration. Regressing two independent random walks of length 50 against each other, 100 times, the statistic $S = |\hat\beta_1|/SE(\hat\beta_1)$ should average around 0.8 and exceed the usual cutoff of 2.0 about 5% of the time if the classical assumptions held. Instead the average $S$ was 4.5, and the null was wrongly rejected on roughly three quarters of all trials. Reaching an honest 5% error rate would require a critical value of 11.2, not 2.0.

The larger simulation regresses $Y_t$ on $m$ independent series, $m = 1$ to $5$, comparing levels against first differences for both random walks and ARIMA$(0,1,1)$ processes. For random walk levels, the false rejection rate climbed from 76% at $m=1$ to 96% at $m=5$, with average $R^2$ rising alongside it and Durbin-Watson $d$ staying low throughout. For the differenced version of the same series, which is just white noise, the false rejection rate dropped back to the honest 4 to 10% range and $d$ sat near 2. The ARIMA$(0,1,1)$ changes, which reduce to an MA(1) process rather than pure white noise, only partially recovered, settling around twice the nominal rate, since there is still one period of genuine leftover correlation. Their summary line is unambiguous: with random walk or near random walk variables, finding a spurious significant relationship is the rule rather than the exception, not an occasional embarrassment.

## What to do about it

Their strongest stated conclusion is close to absolute: if a regression on economic variables shows strongly autocorrelated residuals, the only conclusion that can be reached is that the equation is mis-specified, whatever the value of $R^2$. Mis-specification can come from omitting a relevant variable, including an irrelevant one, or simply having the wrong error structure, usually some mix of the three.

Their real proposal, which they describe as still unfinished, is to build a separate univariate ARIMA model for each series and relate only the leftover, genuinely unpredictable residuals to each other, since a regression should be explaining the part of $Y_t$ that its own past cannot already explain. In the meantime their practical recommendation is to first difference any variable that looks highly autocorrelated, though they are explicit that this is not a universal cure, just a useful rule for the common case of smooth, highly autocorrelated economic series. They illustrate the stakes with a real example, not a simulation: Sheppard's regression of UK consumption on expenditure and money stock gave $\bar R^2 = 0.99$ and $d = 0.59$ in levels, but $\bar R^2 = -0.03$ and $d = 2.21$ in changes. The entire relationship evaporates once differenced.

A referee had suggested that structural models grounded in real economic theory, with few right hand side variables, should be safer from all of this than blind data mined regressions. Granger and Newbold concede some force to the argument in principle but push back hard in practice, because it depends on what counts as a good theory. To a time series analyst a good theory is one whose fitted residuals are genuine white noise, an empirically checkable standard. Some econometricians instead treat a theory as good if it appears inherently correct and therefore does not need testing. Most economic theories say nothing about the correct lag structure or about what the residuals should look like, so even a theoretically motivated, genuinely exogenous variable can still be dragged into this same trap if it is regressed in levels against another non-stationary series. A very high $R^2$ also leaves no room to test whether more variables should be added, forcing blind trust in the theory, whereas working in changes typically yields lower, genuinely testable $R^2$ values.

## Replicating Table 1 myself

After going through the paper I wanted to see the effect directly rather than just read about it, so I wrote a small simulation in [spurious_regression_simulation.ipynb](spurious_regression_simulation.ipynb) that reconstructs the core experiment behind their Table 1.

The setup: a random walk of length $T$ is nothing more than the running sum of independent standard normal shocks, $X_t = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_t$, so it can be generated in one line as the cumulative sum of `T` draws from $N(0,1)$. I generated two such random walks completely independently of each other, then ran an ordinary OLS regression of one on the other using `statsmodels`, and recorded the resulting $R^2$ and the t-statistic on the slope coefficient. Repeating this 100 times, with $T=100$, gave:

| | Paper's Table 1/2 ($T=50$) | My simulation ($T=100$) |
|---|---|---|
| Average $\lvert t\rvert$ for the slope | 4.5 | 5.86 |
| Average $R^2$ | ~0.26 | 0.24 |
| % of trials with $\lvert t\rvert > 2$ | ~75% | 79% |

All three numbers land close to what Granger and Newbold reported, using nothing but two streams of pure random numbers with zero true relationship between them. A single regression I printed out along the way had $R^2 = 0.361$ sitting next to a Durbin-Watson statistic of $0.199$, which is the exact high-$R^2$, low-$d$ signature the paper opens with, appearing in my own output on effectively the first real run.

## Reflection

Coming into this paper I already knew $R^2$ as essentially a variance explained statistic, but I had never really sat with the Durbin-Watson statistic beyond knowing it existed. Working through the formula and the approximation $d \approx 2(1-\rho)$ made it click that it is really just a direct check for whether the residuals are correlated with themselves one period back, nothing more exotic than that, and that a value near zero is the specific signature of an error term that wanders rather than bounces.

The part I initially got wrong, and had to be corrected on, was thinking the spurious correlation between two persistent series was some kind of grid search or cherry picking phenomenon, similar to the backtest overfitting paper. It is not. The expected $R^2$ of $0.47$ between two independent AR(1) series at $\phi=0.9$ is what you get from a single honest regression, run once, with no selection involved at all. That distinction matters to me because it means the usual defenses against p-hacking, like disclosing how many trials you ran, do nothing here. The problem is baked into the statistical properties of the two individual series before any regression is ever run.

I also assumed, wrongly, that this kind of spurious result should fade out with more data, the way ordinary sampling noise does. It was useful to be corrected on this too. Because a random walk's variance grows with $t$ rather than settling down, more observations do not make two unrelated wandering series any less likely to fool you, and the later literature apparently shows the opposite, that the t-statistics involved actually diverge as the sample grows rather than converging to something sensible. That inverted my intuition about what "more data" is supposed to buy you.

The question I found most clarifying to ask out loud was whether this whole argument implied exogenous variables should not be included in a regression. Working through the answer helped me separate two things I had been quietly conflating: exogeneity is about causal direction, whether a variable is truly determined outside the system, while what this paper is actually about is persistence over time, whether a series is stationary or near a unit root. A variable can be perfectly exogenous, genuinely causally related to $Y$, and still produce broken standard errors and an untrustworthy t-statistic if it is non-stationary and regressed in levels. The paper is not arguing against theory driven variables, it is arguing that theory alone does not grant immunity from a purely mechanical problem in the statistics, which is presumably part of why this paper is treated as the direct ancestor of cointegration, the later idea that two non-stationary series can have a genuine, testable long run relationship, just not one you can detect with an ordinary levels regression and a glance at $R^2$.

Writing the simulation myself added something the reading alone did not. Most of the bugs I hit were small but revealing about how easy it is to accidentally hide this exact effect rather than see it. My first attempt at averaging the t-statistics across the 100 trials came out oddly small, and the reason was that I was averaging the raw, signed values, not their absolute values. Since there is no true relationship, the sign of the spurious coefficient in any given trial is essentially arbitrary, so large positive and large negative t-statistics were quietly cancelling out in the average, making the effect look far weaker than it actually was. Only once I took $\lvert t \rvert$ first, matching how the paper actually defines its statistic $S = \lvert\hat\beta_1\rvert / SE(\hat\beta_1)$, did the average jump up to something close to their reported 4.5. That was a useful, slightly humbling lesson in how a perfectly reasonable-looking line of code can silently launder away the very phenomenon you are trying to measure.

The other thing that stuck with me from actually running this rather than just reading about it was seeing the $R^2=0.361$, $d=0.199$ pair show up in my own regression output on one of the very first trials I printed, completely unprompted, from two arrays of pure random numbers I had generated myself thirty seconds earlier. Reading the paper's claim that this pattern is common in published applied work is one thing. Watching it appear immediately, by accident, in a regression I built from scratch with no economic content in it whatsoever, made the warning feel far less abstract.

If I had to compress the whole paper into one line I would keep, it would be that a high $R^2$ and a significant t-statistic are not evidence of anything when the underlying series are persistent, they are close to the default outcome you should expect from noise alone, and the only way to tell the difference is to look at whether the leftover residual actually behaves like noise in the first place.
